# Evidence Quality Rubric

## Source Tiers

### Tier A: Strong Evidence

Use as primary support:

- Peer-reviewed research with clear methods
- Official statistics or regulatory documents
- Standards or technical specifications
- Primary legal or policy sources
- Original datasets
- Audited filings
- Official product or API documentation
- Reproducible benchmark reports

### Tier B: Useful Evidence

Use with context:

- High-quality reviews
- Scholarly books
- Reputable technical articles
- Expert reports with transparent methods
- Analyst reports with disclosed assumptions
- Credible news with primary-source links

### Tier C: Context Only

Use cautiously:

- Vendor marketing
- Blog posts
- Opinion essays
- Forum posts
- Social media
- Unsourced market commentary

### Tier D: Exclude or Flag

Do not use as support unless the topic is specifically about the source:

- Fabricated or unverifiable citations
- Broken links with no archive
- Anonymous claims without evidence
- AI-generated summaries without sources
- Misleading abstracts or citation spam
- Sources with severe conflict of interest and no corroboration

## Scoring Criteria

Score each included source from 0 to 3 on:

- Relevance
- Authority
- Recency
- Method transparency
- Independence
- Reproducibility
- Citation integrity
- Applicability

Suggested total:

- 18 to 24: high confidence source
- 12 to 17: medium confidence source
- 6 to 11: low confidence source
- 0 to 5: context only or exclude

## Confidence Labels

High confidence:

- Multiple Tier A or Tier B sources converge.
- Methods are transparent.
- No major unresolved contradictions.

Medium confidence:

- Evidence is credible but limited.
- Some disagreement or context mismatch exists.
- More data could change the answer.

Low confidence:

- Few sources.
- Weak methods.
- Strong conflict of interest.
- Contradictory findings.
- Fast-changing topic.

Insufficient evidence:

- The available evidence cannot support a responsible answer.

## Red Flags

- A source cites a paper that cannot be found.
- A claim depends on a number with no method.
- A source generalizes from a narrow sample.
- A vendor source makes comparative claims without data.
- The source is outdated for a fast-changing topic.
- The source uses vague phrases such as "studies show" without citations.
- The agent cannot access enough context to verify the claim.
